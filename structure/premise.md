# Premise

Premise is not concept. It is not a logline, not a summary, not the one-word theme on the back cover. A concept is a situation — *what if a chemistry teacher started cooking meth?* A premise is an **argument**: a claim about how the world works that the whole story exists to prove. The concept asks a question. The premise answers it, and the story is the evidence.

Everything else in the book is downstream of this. Get the premise wrong and no amount of structural skill will save the draft — you'll be building a beautiful machine to prove the wrong thing.

## Three Things People Call "Premise"

The word is overloaded. Untangling it is half the work.

- **Concept** — the hook, the *what if*. "A heist, but the target is a dream." This sells the book. It does not organize it.
- **Central dramatic question** — the spine of suspense. *Will Frodo destroy the Ring? Will Stevens admit he loves Miss Kenton?* This pulls the reader forward, page to page. It's answered at the climax.
- **Thematic argument** — the claim the story proves about life. *Loyalty to a corrupt institution costs a man his soul.* This is what the reader carries out the door.

A finished novel needs all three, and they must be the same story seen from three distances. When writers say their premise feels "thin," it's almost always because they have a concept and have mistaken it for an argument.

## The Premise as Thesis (Egri)

Lajos Egri, in *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946), defined premise as a **thesis stated as cause and effect** — a complete sentence containing character, conflict, and conclusion in compressed form:

- "Great love defies even death." → *Romeo and Juliet*
- "Ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction." → *Macbeth*
- "Blind trust leads to destruction." → *King Lear*
- "Poverty encourages crime." → *Dead End*

Egri's test: a real premise *names its own ending*. "Great love defies even death" promises lovers, an obstacle severe enough to involve death, and a love that outlasts it. The premise is a seed with the whole plant folded inside it. If your premise doesn't imply how the story ends, it isn't a premise yet — it's a topic.

This is why "a story about jealousy" is not a premise. Jealousy is a subject. Egri's premise for *Othello* — *"Jealousy destroys itself and the object of its love"* — is a premise: it has a verb, a direction, and a body count.

## The Premise as Charged Outcome (McKee)

Robert McKee's *Story* names the same thing the **Controlling Idea**, and adds the most useful refinement: it has two components — a **value** and a **cause**.

> *Value* + *cause* = "[Final condition of life] **because** [the action that brought it about]."

- "Justice is restored (positive) because an ordinary man refuses to look away (cause)."
- "Passion, naively indulged, leads to self-loathing and death (negative) because the lovers mistook cruelty for desire (cause)." → *Dangerous Liaisons*

The **charge** matters. McKee's point is that the value lands at the climax with a positive or negative sign, and that sign *is* the meaning. A love story that ends with love winning argues one thing; the identical plot ending with love lost argues the opposite. You cannot know your premise until you know the charge of your ending.

## The Premise Has an Enemy

A premise worth proving is **arguable** — which means a smart, sympathetic person could believe the opposite. That opposite is the **counter-premise**, and it is almost always what the antagonist embodies (McKee: *idea vs. counter-idea*).

- *Crime and Punishment.* Premise: **no one is exempt from moral law.** Counter-premise (Raskolnikov's, at the start): *extraordinary men are above morality.* The novel is 500 pages of the counter-premise being tested to destruction. Raskolnikov doesn't argue the premise — he argues *against* it, and loses.
- *The Godfather.* Premise: in protecting his family, a man destroys his soul. Counter-premise: family justifies anything. Michael believes the counter-premise. The film proves the premise on his body.

A premise with no credible counter-premise is propaganda. The reader feels lectured because no real argument is happening — the dice were loaded. The strongest stories let the counter-premise win battles, sometimes most of them, before the premise wins the war.

## How Premise Generates Structure

This is the practical payoff, and it runs *backward*.

Because a true premise names its ending, it tells you what the **climax** must prove. The climax is the premise's courtroom — the moment the argument is decided, on the evidence, in front of the reader. Once you know what the climax must prove, you know:

- what the **counter-premise** is, which tells you who the antagonist must be;
- what condition the protagonist must start in — usually *believing the counter-premise* (see `craft/character` → The Misbelief; the misbelief is the premise's argument made personal);
- what Act I must **plant** so the climax can pay it off (see `structure/act-structure` → The Setup–Payoff Spine).

Premise is the reason structure is engineered backward. You often can't write the opening correctly until you know what the ending has to prove.

It also works as a **filter**, the thematic-level twin of the misbelief filter that drives scenes. Hold a candidate scene against the premise: *does this advance, complicate, or test the argument?* Scenes that prove nothing about the premise are why middles sag — they're motion without evidence. The premise tells you not just what to write but what to **cut**.

## Premise vs. Logline

A logline is a *marketing* compression of the concept and the central dramatic question — "A burned-out hitman comes out of retirement to avenge his dog." It exists to make someone want the story. The premise is the *engineering* compression — the thematic claim and its charge. You pitch with the logline. You build with the premise. Confusing the two produces books that sound great in a sentence and collapse at the midpoint, because a hook was asked to do a load-bearing job it was never shaped for.

## Premise Drift

The most common failure in a finished draft is not the *absence* of a premise but a **mismatch** between the premise the writer intended and the one the story actually argues. The author meant to prove "love conquers all"; the events on the page actually demonstrate "love is a delusion people use to avoid being alone." The draft feels muddy or dishonest, and the writer can't say why.

The fix is diagnostic: read the climax and ask what value lands, with what charge, *because of what cause*. That sentence is your real premise — the one the story is arguing whether you authorized it or not. Then decide: rewrite the ending to match your intended premise, or rewrite the rest to honor the better premise the draft discovered. Drafts often find a truer premise than the outline did. Listen to them.

## Common Premise Failures

**The one-word theme.** "It's about redemption." Redemption is a subject, not a claim. *What about* redemption? "Redemption is possible only after you stop trying to earn it" — now there's a thesis a story can prove.

**The concept in a premise costume.** "What if AI became conscious?" is a generator of situations, not an argument. It tells you nothing about what the ending must prove. Bolt a value and a charge onto it.

**The unfalsifiable premise.** "Family is important." No one disagrees; there's no counter-premise; nothing is at stake. A premise must be deniable to be worth defending.

**The premise that doesn't reach the ending.** "A detective hunts a killer." Where does it *go*? A premise that doesn't imply its conclusion can't tell you how to end the book, which is exactly the help a premise exists to give.

**Two premises fighting.** The A-plot proves one thing, the subplot proves its opposite, and neither is intended as irony. The book feels like it doesn't know what it believes — because it doesn't.

## From the Masters

**The Great Gatsby.** Concept: a mysterious millionaire throws parties to win back a lost love. Central dramatic question: will Gatsby get Daisy back? Premise: **the pursuit of an idealized past destroys the present that might have been lived.** Every structural choice serves it — the green light, the unattended parties, the unchanged shirt of an old photograph. Fitzgerald never states the premise; he proves it, and the famous last line ("borne back ceaselessly into the past") is the verdict, not the theme.

**Breaking Bad.** Counter-premise (Walter's, and the one the show lets win for four seasons): *a man providing for his family is justified in anything.* Premise (proved in the finale, when he finally says "I did it for me. I liked it. I was good at it"): **pride disguised as love destroys everything it claims to protect.** The series is a five-year demolition of the counter-premise its protagonist keeps asserting.

**A Christmas Carol.** Egri-style premise: **no one is beyond redemption** — its own charged, arguable claim, with Marley as the counter-evidence (a man who *was* beyond it) that makes Scrooge's salvation cost something. The three-spirit structure is simply the premise's argument staged in three movements: the cause of the stinginess, its present price, its final wage. Structure as proof.

## Diagnostic Questions

1. State your premise as one sentence with a verb and an outcome. If you can't, you have a concept or a topic, not a premise.
2. What is the *charge* of your ending — positive or negative? Does every major scene bend toward that charge?
3. What's the counter-premise? Who in the story believes it? Does it ever win?
4. Read your actual climax: what value lands, because of what cause? Is that the premise you meant to write?
5. Could a thoughtful person believe the opposite of your premise? If not, there's no argument and no story.
6. Does your premise name its own ending — could a stranger guess roughly how the book ends from the premise alone? It should.
