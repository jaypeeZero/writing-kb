# Scene Structure — Worked Examples

How a single scene is engineered: a goal meets conflict, the situation turns, and the scene enters late and leaves early so only the live part is on the page.

## The proposal that turns mid-scene — Austen's Darcy

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, turning-point, conflict, dialogue
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), ch. 34 — Darcy proposes to Elizabeth at the Hunsford parsonage; she refuses

> In vain I have struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.

**Why it works / How it's built:** The scene has a clear goal (Darcy wants to win Elizabeth) and an opposing goal (Elizabeth wants him gone). Darcy's opening line states his goal aloud — but he spends the proposal insulting her family, so the very speech meant to secure her drives her off. The turn is precise: Elizabeth pivots from cold civility to open attack the instant he mentions Jane and Wickham, and the scene's emotional charge inverts — the suitor becomes the accused. Austen exits the moment the refusal lands; she does not linger on Darcy leaving the room, because the scene's job (rupture, and the seeds of both characters' growth) is done.

**In your own work:** Is there a version of your scene where the thing your character does to win is the same thing that loses it? Austen makes the proposal itself the self-sabotage and exits the moment the refusal lands; you might keep the goal and its undoing further apart, or let the scene breathe past the turn. The test is whether the reversal is built into the character's own choices rather than imposed by luck — and whether anything after the turn is still doing work.

## So now get up — Mantel drops us into the middle of violence

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, pacing, tension, premise
**Source:** Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall* (2009) — the opening scene: young Thomas Cromwell felled in the yard by his drunk father Walter

> "So now get up."

**Why it works / How it's built:** Mantel enters as late as a scene can be entered — after the blow has already landed. The first words are an order shouted down at a boy bleeding on the cobbles; we get the aftermath of the fight, not its setup, so the reader is reconstructing the conflict in real time. The scene's tension is bodily and immediate (Cromwell's eye, the smell of the yard, the boot near his face), and it leaves early too: it gives us just enough of Walter's brutality and Cromwell's survival instinct to seed the entire novel, then cuts away before the boy even stands fully. We learn the premise — this is a man forged by needing to get back up — without a word of explanation.

**In your own work:** How late could your scene start before the reader loses their footing — and is that disorientation actually an asset here? Mantel begins after the blow, on the aftermath, so the reader reconstructs the fight from its wreckage; that is one solution, not the only one — some scenes need the setup to earn the payoff. The consideration is what the reader can infer without being told, and whether withholding the cause makes the moment more alive or merely confusing.

## The restaurant scene — Michael Corleone's point of no return (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, turning-point, tension, arc
**Source:** *The Godfather* (1972, dir. Coppola; screenplay Puzo & Coppola) — Michael meets Sollozzo and police captain McCluskey at Louis' Restaurant

**Why it works / How it's built:** The scene runs on a buried goal the antagonists can't see: Michael's stated goal is to negotiate peace; his real goal is to kill both men with the gun Clemenza's people have taped behind the toilet tank. Conflict is dramatized as surface courtesy over lethal intent — Sollozzo talks business in Italian, McCluskey eats veal, and the suspense is entirely in whether Michael can retrieve the gun and steel himself. The turn is the trip to the men's room: Michael comes back a different person. The kills are the scene's exit; the script does not show him fleeing or the fallout — it cuts on the act, because the scene's true subject (Michael crossing from war hero to killer) is complete the moment he pulls the trigger. The scene enters late (we skip the journey to the restaurant) and leaves early (we skip the escape), so only the live part is on the page.

**In your own work:** Does your viewpoint character want something the other people in the scene can't see — and where does that gap live? The Godfather hides a lethal intent under business courtesy and exits on the irreversible act; you might let the secret surface, or place the exit before or after the deed. The question is whether the distance between the surface and the truth is itself the source of tension, and what the scene's real subject is so you know exactly when it has finished.

## The prologue that spoils the murder — Tartt enters at the end

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, tension, premise, pacing
**Source:** Donna Tartt, *The Secret History* (1992) — the prologue, Richard recalling the killing of Bunny

> The snow in the mountains was melting and Bunny had been dead for several weeks before we came to understand the gravity of our situation.

**Why it works / How it's built:** Tartt builds a whole opening scene around a single withheld moment — the murder — and enters it at the latest possible point: after the body, after the act, in retrospect. The prologue's "scene" is the shove that sends Bunny into the ravine, but it is framed so we know the outcome before the action, which converts the rest of the novel into a slow walk toward a known catastrophe. The scene's conflict is internal (Richard's complicity and dread), and it exits almost immediately, refusing to explain who these people are or why they did it. The unanswered "how did they get here?" is the engine that pulls the reader through 500 pages.

**In your own work:** What would your story gain or lose if the reader already knew the outcome? Tartt shows the result of the murder up front and withholds the cause, converting the novel into a walk toward a known catastrophe; that is one option, and plenty of stories thrive on the opposite — guarding the outcome entirely. Weigh whether suspense for you lives in "what happens" or in "how they got there," because the answer decides where the withheld piece belongs.

## The interrogation as a duel of patience — le Carré's Smiley (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, tension, conflict, subtext
**Source:** John le Carré, *Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy* (1974) — George Smiley's questioning of the captured/turned figures and his debriefs (e.g. the long sessions with Jerry Westerby, Connie Sachs, and the climactic confrontation)

**Why it works / How it's built:** A le Carré interrogation scene has almost no physical action; the goal/conflict structure is purely informational. Smiley's goal is a specific admission; the other party's goal is to give as little as possible. The scene advances in small turns — each disclosure shifts the balance of who knows what, and le Carré marks these turns through the smallest physical tells (a hesitation, a poured drink, the cleaning of Smiley's glasses on his tie) rather than raised voices. He enters these scenes mid-conversation, skipping the pleasantries, and leaves the instant the one needed piece of information has changed hands, often before the interviewee realizes what they have given away. The dramatic stakes are entirely subtextual: the reader tracks the secret war beneath the polite talk.

**In your own work:** Can a scene with no physical action still have opposed goals and real turns? Le Carré makes the currency information — one party wants an admission, the other wants to surrender as little as possible — and marks each shift with a tiny physical tell; you might externalize the conflict more, or trust the reader to track it with even fewer signals. The thing to test is whether each beat actually changes the balance of who knows what, and whether your physical details are carrying the turns or just decorating them.

## A conversation that detonates a marriage — Eliot's Dorothea and Casaubon (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** scene-structure, turning-point, conflict, character
**Source:** George Eliot, *Middlemarch* (1871–72) — the scene where Dorothea, newly returning to her husband's study, asks Casaubon whether he wants her to continue his life's work after his death

**Why it works / How it's built:** The scene has a deceptively small goal — Dorothea simply wants an answer to a sincere question — but it collides with Casaubon's terror about his unfinished, worthless scholarship, so the request lands as an accusation he cannot bear. Eliot builds the conflict through Dorothea's generosity meeting Casaubon's wounded pride: the kinder she is, the more he reads it as pity, which is unendurable to him. The turn comes when his cold reply makes Dorothea's offered devotion curdle into the first true resentment of her marriage; the affection she came in with leaves the room. Eliot enters after the question has formed and exits on the silence that follows his rebuff, the marriage permanently altered in a single quiet exchange.

**In your own work:** Could the most generous thing one of your characters offers be the one thing the other cannot bear to receive? Eliot lets Dorothea's kindness read to Casaubon as pity, so the goodwill she enters with is gone by the exit; you might find the same rupture in a cruelty, a silence, or a misread gesture. The consideration is what each character secretly fears, because a turn lands hardest when an innocent act strikes exactly there — and the scene is over when the emotional charge they came in with has inverted.
