# Detail Selection — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages with the real text embedded: each shows how one chosen detail (or a tight cluster) carries weight that summary or a pile of description never could.

## The mundane notice that names the wound it conceals

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, subtext, economy, show-dont-tell
**Source:** Jhumpa Lahiri, "A Temporary Matter," *Interpreter of Maladies* (1999) — the opening of a story about a marriage hollowed out by a stillbirth

> The notice informed them that it was a temporary matter: for five days their electricity would be cut off for one hour, beginning at eight P.M. A line had gone down in the last snowstorm, and the repairmen were going to take advantage of the milder evenings to set it right. The work would affect only the houses on the quiet tree-lined street, within walking distance of a row of brick-faced stores and a trolley stop, where Shoba and Shukumar had lived for three years.

**Why it works:** Lahiri opens on the single most ordinary detail imaginable — a utility notice — and lets its bureaucratic phrase, "a temporary matter," become the load-bearing irony of the whole story. The repair, the snowstorm, the milder evenings are chosen because they are trivial: the couple's real damage is permanent and unspoken, and the contrast between the fixable outage and the unfixable marriage does all the thematic work without a word of commentary. The detail is selected for what it can quietly stand in for.

**In your own work:** What small, fixable problem could sit in the foreground of your scene while the real, unfixable trouble stays unspoken? Lahiri lets a utility notice and its bureaucratic phrasing carry that irony; you might let a different mundane thing do it, or choose not to lean on irony at all. The question to ask is whether the surface detail is ordinary enough to pass unnoticed yet precise enough that its language quietly names the wound.

## The turtle's back legs: synecdoche for a whole displaced people

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, show-dont-tell, economy
**Source:** John Steinbeck, *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) — the interchapter in which a land turtle crosses a highway

> The back legs went to work, straining like elephant legs, and the shell tipped to an angle so that the front legs could not reach the cement plain.

**Why it works:** Steinbeck spends an entire interchapter on one turtle dragging itself across a road, and chooses the most physical, effortful detail he can — legs "straining like elephant legs," the doomed tilt of the shell — so the small creature's blind persistence becomes the migrants' persistence. The detail is selected for transferability: every reader will later map the turtle's grind onto the Joads' journey without being told to. Nothing is allegorized on the page; the concrete image is left to resonate.

**In your own work:** Is there something small in your world enacting, in miniature, the larger struggle your story is about? Steinbeck found a turtle and rendered it with relentless physical effort while refusing to gloss the parallel; that is one solution, not the only one — some stories want the resonance closer to the surface, or not isolated in an interchapter at all. The consideration is whether the concrete image is specific enough to resonate on its own, so the reader maps the meaning without being told to.

## Naming each raindrop: the detail that renders a state of mind

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, voice, show-dont-tell
**Source:** Denis Johnson, "Car Crash While Hitchhiking," *Jesus' Son* (1992) — the addict-narrator waiting at a freeway on-ramp

> The downpour raked the asphalt and gurgled in the ruts. My thoughts zoomed pitifully. The travelling salesman had fed me pills that made the linings of my veins feel scraped out. My jaw ached. I knew every raindrop by its name.

**Why it works:** Johnson loads the passage with ordinary weather and bodily detail — gurgling ruts, an aching jaw, scraped-out veins — and then ends on one impossible, hallucinatory observation: "I knew every raindrop by its name." That single chosen detail tells you the narrator is high more efficiently than any explanation of the drugs could, because it shows the world as his chemistry has rearranged it. The selected detail isn't description; it's a window onto consciousness.

**In your own work:** How would your character's altered or extreme state actually rearrange what they perceive? Johnson conveys the high through a single impossible observation — knowing every raindrop by name — rather than describing the drug; you might reach for a different distortion, or a quieter one. The test is whether the perception shows the state from inside rather than reporting it, so the detail becomes a window onto the consciousness instead of a label on it.

## The unravelling boot-stitch: a beating told through one thing the victim can see

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, show-dont-tell, tension
**Source:** Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall* (2009) — the novel's opening, the teenage Thomas Cromwell beaten by his father

> Blood from the gash on his head — which was his father's first effort — is trickling across his face. Add to this, his left eye is blinded; but if he squints sideways, with his right eye he can see that the stitching of his father's boot is unravelling.

**Why it works:** Mantel could have catalogued every blow; instead she narrows the entire violence to what the boy's one working eye can register — the unravelling stitch of the boot that is kicking him. The detail is chosen for proximity and helplessness: it puts us flat on the cobbles at the level of a child's downed face, and the loose thread tells us this is a poor, hard, repeated household without a syllable of summary. Restricting the detail to the victim's literal sightline is what makes it unbearable.

**In your own work:** In a violent or chaotic moment, what can your character actually perceive from the position they have been forced into? Mantel restricts the whole beating to one fraying boot-stitch at the boy's eye level; that is one way to use the limitation, and another scene might widen the lens or shift whose senses we share. The consideration is whether the constrained sightline does more work than a full catalogue of the action would — whether the limit itself generates the intimacy and the dread.
