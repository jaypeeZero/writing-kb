# Premise — Worked Examples

The generative "what if" at a work's core: how a premise is stated, then systematically established and exploited. Verified opening text where the premise is announced in a single stroke.

## The premise as the first sentence: *The Metamorphosis*

**Demonstrates:** premise, stakes, scene-structure, tension
**Source:** Franz Kafka, *The Metamorphosis* (1915) — traveling salesman Gregor Samsa, trans. variously

> One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin.

**How it's built:** Kafka delivers the entire "what if" in the opening line and then refuses to explain it — there is no cause, no magic, no science, just the fact, reported in the same flat tone one would use for an overslept alarm. The work is in the *exploitation*: having spent his fantastic premise instantly, Kafka pours all his realism into the mundane consequences — how Gregor will get to work, how the family will pay the debts, who will bring food, the disgust that curdles into neglect. The transformation is a given; the drama is bureaucratic and domestic. By treating the impossible as a settled fact and the ordinary as the problem, Kafka makes the metaphor (a worker reduced to a useless body, then discarded) do the work without ever stating it.

**In your own work:** How much of your premise needs explaining, and how much can simply be granted? Kafka states the impossible as a flat fact, never names a cause, and pours all his realism into the mundane fallout — but announcing it in the first line is one option, and some premises do want their mechanism. The principle to weigh is that the more ordinary the consequences feel, the more readily a reader accepts an extraordinary cause.

## A premise that rewires how the whole book tells time: *One Hundred Years of Solitude*

**Demonstrates:** premise, scene-structure, turning-point
**Source:** Gabriel García Márquez, *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (1967), trans. Gregory Rabassa — the Buendía family and the town of Macondo

> Many years later, as he faced the firing squad, Colonel Aureliano Buendía was to remember that distant afternoon when his father took him to discover ice.

**How it's built:** The opening sentence establishes the book's generative premise not of plot but of *time*: past, present, and future collapse into a single breath — a future firing squad, a present act of remembering, and a childhood marvel (ice as wonder) all in one line. From there Márquez exploits it relentlessly: prophecies arrive before the events they predict, generations repeat the same names and fates, and the founding miracle (ice) and the recurring doom (the firing squad) braid through a hundred years. The premise — that in Macondo time is cyclical and memory is prophecy — licenses every magical event that follows. The line teaches the reader how to read the book.

**In your own work:** Does your premise govern only what happens, or also how the work tells time and reality? Márquez collapses past, present, and future into one opening breath so the reader learns the world's clock runs differently — but a premise can reset the rules early or reveal them gradually, and not every story wants its physics declared in line one. Consider whether your opening teaches the reader how to read the rest, or only what to expect next.

## A premise that is a confession of fabrication: the "Cool Girl" engine of *Gone Girl*

**Demonstrates:** premise, subtext, stakes, turning-point
**Source:** Gillian Flynn, *Gone Girl* (2012) — Amy Dunne's mid-novel reveal that her diary, and her disappearance, were staged

> Being the Cool Girl means I am a hot, brilliant, funny woman who adores football, poker, dirty jokes, and burping, who plays video games, drinks cheap beer, loves threesomes and anal sex... while somehow maintaining a size 2, because Cool Girls are above all hot.

**How it's built:** The novel's true premise is hidden behind a fake one. For half the book the reader trusts Amy's sympathetic diary; the mid-book "Cool Girl" monologue detonates it, revealing that the woman we pitied invented a persona to trap her husband and faked her own murder to punish him. The premise — *a marriage as a pair of dueling, performed fictions* — is established by the structure itself: two unreliable narrators, his account versus her diary, neither true. Flynn exploits it by making every earlier scene reinterpretable; the reader has to re-read the whole first half once the premise is exposed. The monologue is the thesis statement: identity in this book is a costume worn to be loved, and resented for the wearing.

**In your own work:** Is there a true premise you could hide beneath one the reader willingly accepts? Flynn lets a sympathetic diary stand in for the real story, then detonates it at the midpoint so the whole first half becomes reinterpretable — but the reveal could come earlier or later, and the payoff need not be a literal re-read. Ask whether every earlier scene genuinely supports both readings, since a buried premise only rewards the reader if the surface one was honestly built.

## The "what if" of a moral thought-experiment: "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas"

**Demonstrates:** premise, stakes, subtext, turning-point
**Source:** Ursula K. Le Guin, "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973) — a utopian city whose joy depends on one child's suffering

**How it's built:** Le Guin's premise is a philosophical bargain made literal: the perfect happiness of an entire city — its festivals, its beauty, its kindness — is guaranteed only so long as one child is kept starving and filthy in a locked basement, and everyone knows it. She establishes the premise by first lavishing pages on Omelas's joy (daring the reader to disbelieve a happy place), then revealing the cost, then forcing the question: knowing this, do you stay? The exploitation is the title's act — a few citizens, after seeing the child, simply walk out of the city, toward a place "even less imaginable to most of us than the city of happiness." The story spends almost no plot; the premise *is* the story, a trap that implicates the reader's own bargains.

**In your own work:** Could your premise pose a moral bargain rather than tell a plot? Le Guin names exactly what one child pays for everyone else's happiness and refuses to resolve it, leaving the reader's discomfort as the intended effect — but a thought-experiment premise can resolve, partially resolve, or stay open, and yours may want more plot than hers. The consideration is whether the unease you leave behind is the point, or an accident of an ending you haven't found.

## A premise that inverts a familiar world: *The Left Hand of Darkness*

**Demonstrates:** premise, scene-structure, subtext, stakes
**Source:** Ursula K. Le Guin, *The Left Hand of Darkness* (1969) — an envoy on the planet Gethen, whose humans have no fixed sex

**How it's built:** Le Guin's "what if" is anthropological: imagine a human species with no permanent gender — people are sexless most of the time and become male or female only briefly, unpredictably, during "kemmer." She establishes it not as a gimmick but through accumulated consequence: there is no war framed as masculine conquest, no rape culture, no division of labor by sex, and the human envoy Genly Ai keeps misreading the Gethenians through his own gendered assumptions, which is the real drama. The premise is exploited as a mirror — every reader brings binary expectations the book methodically dismantles, so the "alien" exposes the contingency of our own world's arrangements. The slow thaw of Genly's friendship with Estraven is the premise made personal: love that has to invent its own terms.

**In your own work:** Which assumption your reader treats as universal could your premise remove or invert? Le Guin takes fixed gender off the table and works patiently through the social consequences, using an outsider who keeps misreading the world to turn the premise back on the reader's defaults — but the mirror is one strategy, and the inverted feature could be anything readers take for granted. Ask whether your premise interrogates those defaults or merely decorates the setting with them.
