# Opening Lines — Worked Examples

Self-contained first lines and paragraphs with the real text embedded: each establishes voice, world, or the contract between book and reader in the smallest possible space.

## Voice as seduction: the opening that makes you complicit in its sound

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, voice, prose-rhythm
**Source:** Vladimir Nabokov, *Lolita* (1955) — Humbert Humbert's first words

> Lolita, light of my life, fire of my loins. My sin, my soul. Lo-lee-ta: the tip of the tongue taking a trip of three steps down the palate to tap, at three, on the teeth. Lo. Lee. Ta.

**Why it works:** Before any plot, the opening declares that this book will run on the narrator's intoxicating, dangerous voice — the alliteration ("light," "life," "loins"), the syllable-by-syllable savoring of the name. By making the reader's own tongue perform "Lo-lee-ta," Nabokov implicates us in Humbert's pleasure, establishing the entire moral trap of the novel in its first breath. The contract is set: trust nothing, but you cannot stop listening.

**In your own work:** If voice is the engine of your book, what is the first thing the opening should make the reader feel about that voice? Nabokov makes the reader's own tongue perform the name, so complicity in the sound previews complicity in the story; that is one way an opening can establish its contract, and many books set their terms through situation, image, or withholding instead. The thing to ask is what your first lines teach the reader to do, and whether that is the relationship you want the whole book to run on.

## The mundane notice that hides the real subject

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, subtext, economy
**Source:** Jhumpa Lahiri, "A Temporary Matter," *Interpreter of Maladies* (1999)

> The notice informed them that it was a temporary matter: for five days their electricity would be cut off for one hour, beginning at eight P.M.

**Why it works:** Lahiri opens on pure banality — a utility outage — and seeds the title-phrase "temporary matter" as quiet irony, since the marriage's real damage is anything but temporary. The contract here is one of restraint: the reader is told nothing dramatic and must lean in to sense that the small fixable problem stands in for a large unfixable one. The flatness is a promise that meaning will live beneath the surface.

**In your own work:** What promise does the flatness of an opening make to a reader? Lahiri opens on pure banality and lets a fixable inconvenience quietly stand in for an unfixable one, so the understatement itself signals that meaning will live beneath the surface; you might make a louder promise, or a quieter one, depending on how much you want the reader to lean in. The consideration is whether your first lines accurately advertise where the story's real subject is going to live.

## History welded to weather: a private dread inside a public horror

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, voice, detail-selection
**Source:** Sylvia Plath, *The Bell Jar* (1963)

> It was a queer, sultry summer, the summer they electrocuted the Rosenbergs, and I didn't know what I was doing in New York.

**Why it works:** Plath fuses three things in one sentence — oppressive weather, a real execution, and the narrator's disorientation — so private numbness and public violence bleed together from the start. The electrocution image is planted as foreshadowing for Esther's own electroshock, making the line both atmosphere and structural seed. The contract: this voice will read its own breakdown through the world around it.

**In your own work:** Could a single image in your opening do double duty — atmosphere now, structural seed later? Plath fuses weather, a real execution, and the narrator's disorientation so private numbness and public violence bleed together, and the electrocution image waits to detonate as foreshadowing; that is one way to make an opening detail load-bearing, and a public event is only one source of such an image. The question is whether your first sentence sets mood while quietly planting something the story can return to.

## A whole city conjured by a sound

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, detail-selection, prose-rhythm
**Source:** Ursula K. Le Guin, "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," *The Wind's Twelve Quarters* (1973)

> With a clamor of bells that set the swallows soaring, the Festival of Summer came to the city Omelas, bright-towered by the sea.

**Why it works:** Le Guin builds an entire happy world from two kinetic details — bells ringing, swallows flushed into flight — rather than describing the city directly. The fronted phrase makes the line lift before it lands, so the reader feels the festival's joy as motion. The contract is one of conjured beauty, deliberately staged so the story can later complicate it.

**In your own work:** Can a place's mood be conjured through motion rather than described directly? Le Guin builds a whole happy world from bells ringing and swallows flushed into flight, and the fronted phrase makes the line lift before it lands; that is one way to summon emotional weather, and another opening might reach for stillness, sound, or plain statement instead. The thing to weigh is whether a single kinetic image carries enough of the world's feeling to stand in for direct description.

## A suicide and a punchline: comedy and place in one breath

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, voice, tension
**Source:** Zadie Smith, *White Teeth* (2000)

> Early in the morning, late in the century, Cricklewood Broadway.

**Why it works:** Smith's opening fragment performs a verbal pirouette — "Early in the morning, late in the century" — that announces a playful, expansive, time-and-place-conscious narrator, then grounds it in a specific London street. The clause-balanced wit signals a comic, multigenerational saga to come, while the precise locale promises social texture. The contract: this voice will be witty, panoramic, and rooted in real streets.

**In your own work:** What can the very shape of an opening fragment reveal about the narrator before any plot arrives? Smith's balanced "early in the morning, late in the century" performs a verbal pirouette that announces a witty, panoramic voice, then grounds it on a named street; that symmetry is one way to advertise a narrator's sensibility, and yours might announce itself through bluntness, drift, or a long unspooling sentence instead. The consideration is whether the form of your first line is already characterizing the voice that will tell the whole story.

## The second-person dare: a book that addresses you reading it

**Demonstrates:** opening-lines, voice, economy
**Source:** Italo Calvino, *If on a winter's night a traveler* (1979, trans. William Weaver)

> You are about to begin reading Italo Calvino's new novel, *If on a winter's night a traveler*. Relax. Concentrate. Dispel every other thought.

**Why it works:** Calvino opens by naming the act the reader is performing and issuing clipped imperatives, collapsing the wall between book and reader before any story begins. The second person and the one-word commands establish the novel's entire metafictional game in three sentences. The contract is unusual and explicit: you, the reader, are a character here.

**In your own work:** If your book plays an unconventional game with the reader, how soon and how explicitly should the opening declare its rules? Calvino names the act of reading and issues clipped imperatives, collapsing the wall between book and reader in three sentences; that direct address is one way to set unusual terms, and other books smuggle their rules in obliquely rather than announcing them. The question is whether your opening establishes the contract the rest of the book actually intends to keep.
