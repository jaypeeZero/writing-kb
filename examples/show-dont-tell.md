# Show, Don't Tell — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages: the actual text is embedded so you can study the move without owning the book. Each dramatizes emotion or atmosphere through concrete evidence — physical process, object, image, environment — instead of naming the feeling.

## Atmosphere through process: horror staged as biology

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, detail-selection, prose-rhythm, tension
**Source:** Annie Dillard, *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek* (1974) — the narrator watches a frog at the creek's edge be killed and emptied by a giant water bug

> He was a very small frog with wide, dull eyes. And just as I looked at him, he slowly crumpled and began to sag. The spirit vanished from his eyes as if snuffed. His skin emptied and drooped; his very skull seemed to collapse and settle like a kicked tent. He was shrinking before my eyes like a deflating football. [...] Soon, part of his skin, formless as a pricked balloon, lay in floating folds like bright scum on top of the water: it was a monster.

**Why it works:** Dillard never writes "it was horrifying" or "nature is cruel." She makes us watch, in slow physical detail, a living thing become an empty bag of skin — "a kicked tent," "a deflating football," "a pricked balloon." The dread is manufactured entirely by the verbs (crumpled, sag, snuffed, droop, collapse) and the accelerating similes, each more deflated than the last. The atmosphere of cosmic horror is an emergent property of accurately described biology; the reader supplies the terror because the prose refuses to.

**In your own work:** What physical process could carry the feeling you'd otherwise be tempted to name? Dillard manufactures dread by slowing time and chaining deflating similes — kicked tent, deflating football, pricked balloon — each more emptied than the last, so the reader supplies the horror; but the same restraint applies to wonder, tenderness, or disgust, and your accumulation might build differently. The underlying idea is that accurately rendered process lets emotion emerge rather than be announced, so it's worth asking which feeling you can trust the reader to reach on their own.

## Emotion as object: grief and grace fused into one glowing glass

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, detail-selection, subtext, prose-rhythm
**Source:** James Baldwin, "Sonny's Blues," in *Going to Meet the Man* (1965) — the narrator, reconciled at last with his estranged brother Sonny, sends a drink up to the bandstand as Sonny plays

> Then he sat down again, and he had no expression on his face. But I could see his hand on his thigh, and it was trembling. [...] And, while Sonny played, I saw a glass of scotch and milk on top of the piano. [...] For me, then, as they began to play again, it glowed and shook above my brother's head like the very cup of trembling.

**Why it works:** Years of estrangement, guilt, and reconciliation between two brothers resolve not into a speech but into a single object: a glass of scotch and milk on a piano. By transferring the emotion into a thing the narrator can see ("it glowed and shook"), Baldwin lets the feeling become visible and ungovernable. The biblical echo ("the cup of trembling," from Isaiah) lifts the ordinary glass into a vessel of suffering and forgiveness without the narrator ever explaining what he feels. The trembling hand earlier in the passage prepares it; the trembling cup completes it.

**In your own work:** Is there an ordinary object already in your scene that could hold the feeling a character can't say out loud? Baldwin transfers years of estrangement into a glass of scotch and milk and lets it glow and shake above the bandstand — but the vessel might be a chair, a coat, a sound, and the borrowed resonance need not be biblical as his is. What's worth weighing is whether an image can bear weight a confession would only flatten; an object earns that role when the scene has quietly prepared it, the way the trembling hand readies the trembling cup.

## Atmosphere as enabler: what darkness lets people say

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, subtext, detail-selection, economy
**Source:** Jhumpa Lahiri, "A Temporary Matter," in *Interpreter of Maladies* (1999) — a grieving, estranged couple discovers that nightly hour-long power cuts change something between them

> Something happened when the house was dark. They were able to talk to each other again.

**Why it works:** Lahiri never tells us the marriage is dead, or that the couple can no longer face each other in the light. She shows it by its inverse: the literal dark is the only condition under which they can speak. The environment does the emotional reporting — the fact that honesty requires the lights off tells us everything about the shame and distance they live in by day. Two plain sentences imply an entire failing marriage and the specific shape of its wound.

**In your own work:** Is there an external condition that changes what your characters can say or do — and does that change expose the truth of their ordinary state? Lahiri lets the nightly dark be the only condition under which a dead marriage can speak, so the environment does the reporting; the condition might instead be a long drive, a crowded room, a deadline, anything that shifts the rules. The principle is that a truth no one would state aloud can surface in the gap between how people behave under the special condition and how they behave the rest of the time.

## Showing joy through concrete light, not abstraction

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, detail-selection, voice, prose-rhythm
**Source:** Marilynne Robinson, *Gilead* (2004) — the dying pastor John Ames, writing to his young son, tries to convey what he has loved

> I love the prairie! So often I have seen the dawn come and the light flood over the land and everything turn radiant at once, that word "good" so profoundly affirmed in my soul that I am amazed I should be allowed to witness such a thing.

**Why it works:** Ames could have written "I have led a blessed life" — pure telling. Instead he gives us the specific recurring image that produced the blessing: dawn flooding the prairie, the land turning radiant "all at once." The abstraction ("good," "blessed") is earned because it's anchored to a witnessed, physical event he has seen "so often." The joy is shown as a thing that happens to light on a landscape; the theology rides in on the image rather than replacing it. Even the exclamation mark is evidence — a restrained man's prose breaking open.

**In your own work:** When your character reaches for a large abstraction — joy, awe, gratitude — what specific thing have they actually witnessed that produced it? Robinson lets Ames earn "good" by anchoring it to dawn flooding the prairie, a sight he's seen "so often," so the abstraction rides in on the image; you might anchor it to a single event rather than a repeated one, or let the image stand without the word at all. The order is what matters: a concrete experience can earn an abstract word, but the abstract word rarely conjures a concrete experience in the reader, so it's worth asking which comes first on your page.

## What the dying mind chooses: loss shown by a single saved memory

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, detail-selection, pacing, subtext
**Source:** Tobias Wolff, "Bullet in the Brain," in *The Night in Question* (1996) — Anders, a cynical book critic, is shot in a bank robbery; the story tracks the last instant of his consciousness and lists everything he does *not* remember before landing on the one thing he does

> The bullet is already in the brain; it won't be outrun forever, or charmed to a halt. In the end it will do its work and leave the troubled skull behind, dragging its comet's tail of memory and hope and talent and love into the marble hall of commerce. [...] *They is, they is, they is.*

**Why it works:** Wolff dramatizes a wasted life not by stating it but by showing what the brain reaches for at the end — not Anders's books or loves, but a childhood baseball afternoon and a boy's ungrammatical, joyful phrase, "they is." The whole tragedy of his adult cynicism is carried by the contrast: the one memory he kept is the one moment he loved language for its sound instead of sneering at its rules. By dragging "memory and hope and talent and love" away in a comet's tail, the bullet shows us, physically, everything being lost. The story tells us nothing about regret; it makes us feel it by what survives.

**In your own work:** If your character's mind could keep only one image, which would it choose — and would that choice quietly judge how they spent the rest of their life? Wolff sets a childhood "they is" against an adult life of sneering, and lets the contrast deliver the verdict without comment; the revealing moment need not be death, and your gap might be between what a character claims to value and what they actually protect. The principle is that what a mind reaches for under pressure exposes its real allegiances, so the writer's work is choosing the one detail whose presence indicts the absence of all the others.

## Raw feeling shown as accusation, not confession

**Demonstrates:** show-dont-tell, voice, subtext, economy
**Source:** Denis Johnson, "Car Crash While Hitchhiking," in *Jesus' Son* (1992) — the closing line, the strung-out narrator addressing the reader from a haze of drugs, wreckage, and dimly remembered death

> And you, you ridiculous people, you expect me to help you.

**Why it works:** The narrator never says he is broken, guilty, or beyond reach. He shows it by turning on us — the abrupt swerve into second-person accusation ("you ridiculous people") exposes a mind too damaged to offer comfort and too haunted to pretend otherwise. The emotion isn't stated; it's enacted in the wild, defensive lash of the line. We feel his dereliction precisely because he refuses to confess it and attacks instead. The bitterness is the evidence of the wound.

**In your own work:** When your character is most hurt, where does the feeling go if not into confession? Johnson lets the narrator lash outward — "you ridiculous people" — so the wound is enacted as accusation rather than admitted; the deflection might just as well take the form of a joke, a chore, a sudden change of subject. What's worth noticing is that an emotion the character refuses to name can be dramatized by what they do instead of naming it, and the wrongness of the deflection is often sharper evidence of the wound than any direct statement.
