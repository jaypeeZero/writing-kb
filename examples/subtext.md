# Subtext — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages: the actual text is embedded so you can study the move without owning the book. Each carries its real meaning *beneath* the spoken or stated surface.

## The plain sentence that holds a whole philosophy

**Demonstrates:** subtext, economy, character, show-dont-tell
**Source:** Annie Proulx, "Brokeback Mountain" (1997) — the closing movement of the story, Ennis's stance toward his loss

> There was some open space between what he knew and what he tried to believe, but nothing could be done about it, and if you can't fix it you've got to stand it.

**Why it works:** Ennis Del Mar is inarticulate by nature; he could never *explain* his grief, his closeted love, or his resignation. So Proulx hands him one plainspoken, grammatically rough sentence that accidentally states his entire philosophy of endurance — "stand it," not solve it, not heal it, just bear it. The vernacular flatness is the point: the bigger the feeling, the smaller and blunter the words. Nothing is labeled "repression" or "grief"; the voice carries all of it.

**In your own work:** Does your character have words equal to their feeling — and if not, what blunt, ordinary sentence might state their whole survival without their meaning it to? Proulx hands Ennis one grammatically rough line that holds his entire philosophy of endurance, and the vernacular flatness is the point; but an articulate character would need a different route to subtext, since this move depends on the words being smaller than the feeling. The test is the distance between how much is felt and how little is said: the wider and quieter that gap, the more the plain sentence carries.

## Deflection as confession: the line that means its opposite

**Demonstrates:** subtext, dialogue, show-dont-tell, economy
**Source:** Ernest Hemingway, "Hills Like White Elephants" (1927) — a couple circling, without ever naming it, the question of an abortion

> "They look like white elephants," she said.
> "I've never seen one," the man drank his beer.
> "No, you wouldn't have," she said.

**Why it works:** Not one line is about the actual subject; the word "abortion" never appears in the story. Yet the whole relationship is here — her reaching for the hills as something strange and alive, his literal-minded refusal to follow her there, and the small bitter blade of "No, you wouldn't have," which says *you never see what I see, you never will*. The conflict lives entirely in deflection: the man drinks his beer (avoidance made physical) instead of engaging. Readers infer the power dynamic, the resentment, the fear — none of it stated.

**In your own work:** What is the real subject your characters are circling, and could the whole scene work without ever naming it? Hemingway keeps "abortion" off the page entirely and lets the conflict live in what she reaches for, what he refuses to follow, and one line that lands as a quiet accusation; total avoidance is one setting on the dial, and your scene might name the subject once or glance at it sidelong instead. The principle is that readers will infer a power dynamic from behavior — what's pursued, what's deflected, where the small blade goes in — so it's worth asking how much you can leave unsaid before the meaning stops carrying.
