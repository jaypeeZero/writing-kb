# Pacing — Worked Examples

How real works speed up, slow down, and use white space: a mix of scene-vs-summary control and whole-work tempo architecture, with verified text where a single passage carries the move.

## Decelerating a whole novel onto one disastrous hour: *On Chesil Beach*

**Demonstrates:** pacing, scene-structure, tension, stakes
**Source:** Ian McEwan, *On Chesil Beach* (2007) — newlyweds Edward and Florence on their 1962 wedding night at a Dorset hotel

**How it's built:** McEwan takes a botched wedding-night consummation that lasts minutes and stretches it across most of the book, slowing time almost to a standstill. The present-tense action — a dinner of roast beef carried in by waiters, the move to the bed, a single premature, catastrophic physical moment — is constantly interrupted by deceleration: flashbacks to how the couple met, Florence's buried dread of sex, Edward's pride. The technique is rubato — he expands the seconds that matter (the touch, the recoil, the flight onto the beach) and summarizes the years in a paragraph at the end. The mismatch between clock-time (one evening) and page-time (a novella) *is* the meaning: a whole shared future is decided in an instant neither can talk their way out of.

**In your own work:** Which moment in your story costs more than the clock-time it occupies? McEwan answers the mismatch by expanding seconds across a novella and flooding them with backstory; you might find a different ratio, or carry the disproportion in a single paragraph. The principle to weigh is that page-time and clock-time are separate dials — the gap between them can itself become the meaning.

## Summary that swallows decades in a sentence: *A Visit from the Goon Squad*

**Demonstrates:** pacing, scene-structure, turning-point
**Source:** Jennifer Egan, *A Visit from the Goon Squad* (2010) — interlinked chapters spanning the 1970s punk scene to a near-future New York

**How it's built:** Egan jumps the reader across roughly fifty years and a dozen viewpoints, and her signature pacing trick is the parenthetical flash-forward — a minor character's entire fate compressed into a clause inside a present scene, so a teenager at a concert is suddenly told, in passing, who they will become and how they will die. The book also swings to the opposite pole: a full late chapter ("Great Rock and Roll Pauses") is rendered as a twelve-year-old's PowerPoint slides, where white space and the literal "pause" of an empty slide does the emotional work that prose would over-explain. Egan alternates extreme acceleration (decades per page) with extreme deceleration (a single silence given its own slide).

**In your own work:** When you need the reader to feel the weight of time, will you dramatize it or compress it? Egan does both at once — a fate buried in a parenthetical clause, a silence handed its own PowerPoint slide — but the casual flash-forward is one tool among many, and white space is one way to render absence. Consider whether the emotion you're after lives in event or in omission, and let that decide how much page you spend.

## White space as a beat: the section break that withholds

**Demonstrates:** pacing, white space, tension, subtext
**Source:** Raymond Carver, "Cathedral" (1983) — and Carver's minimalist method generally; here the blind man guides the narrator's hand to draw

**How it's built:** Carver paces with omission. He cuts scenes at the moment before resolution and lets a section break (or just a hard, short sentence) stand in for everything unsaid, forcing the reader to supply the interval. In "Cathedral" the long, slow, almost real-time final passage — the two men's hands moving together on the paper, the narrator's eyes closed — is deliberately under-described; the famous closing beat ("It's really something") is tiny, and the white space after it is enormous. The deceleration into near-silence at the end, after a chatty, defensive narration, is the whole arc of the story made into tempo.

**In your own work:** What does your scene gain if you stop one beat before the resolution? Carver lets a section break and a short flat line carry what a paragraph would over-explain — but cutting early is a wager about your reader's willingness to supply the interval, and not every epiphany wants silence. The consideration is whether omission here trusts the reader or merely strands them.

## Accelerating toward catastrophe: the gathering speed of *The Day of the Triffids*

**Demonstrates:** pacing, tension, stakes, scene-structure
**Source:** John Wyndham, *The Day of the Triffids* (1951) — Bill Masen wakes in hospital, eyes bandaged, the morning after most of humanity has been blinded

**How it's built:** Wyndham opens slow and intimate — one man in a hospital bed, irritated that no one has come to remove his bandages, the city oddly quiet. The pacing is domestic, almost comic. Then he peels the bandages and the aperture widens at an accelerating rate: blind patients, blind staff, a blind city, looting, collapse, the triffids advancing. Each chapter pulls back to a larger ring of disaster, and the tempo quickens as Bill's options narrow. The slow, mundane opening is load-bearing — it buys the acceleration its shock, because we were grounded in an ordinary irritation first.

**In your own work:** What ordinary ground do you stand the reader on before the catastrophe arrives? Wyndham buys his acceleration with a slow, domestic opening, then widens the lens ring by ring as Bill's options narrow — but the quiet opening is load-bearing in his version, not a fixed rule. Ask whether your tempo actually tracks the shrinking of your protagonist's choices, and what the reader needs to feel grounded in first.
