# Dialogue — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages: the actual text is embedded so you can study the move without owning the book. Each shows dialogue doing real work — carrying subtext, conflict, character, or compression — rather than relaying information.

## Dialogue as argument the speakers can't win: love and its definitions

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, subtext, character, tension
**Source:** Raymond Carver, "What We Talk About When We Talk About Love," in the 1981 collection — two couples drinking gin at a kitchen table; Terri describes Ed, the violent ex-boyfriend who preceded her current husband Mel

> "He beat me up one night. He dragged me around the living room by my ankles. He kept saying, 'I love you, I love you, you bitch.' He went on dragging me around the living room. My head kept knocking on things." Terri looked around the table. "What do you do with love like that?"
>
> [...] "My God, don't be silly. That's not love, and you know it," Mel said. "I don't know what you'd call it, but I sure know you wouldn't call it love."
>
> "Say what you want to, but I know it was," Terri said. "It may sound crazy to you, but it's true just the same. People are different, Mel. Sure, sometimes he may have acted crazy. Okay. But he loved me. In his own way maybe, but he loved me. There was love there, Mel."

**Why it works:** The surface is a friendly disagreement about a word; underneath, it's a marriage exposing its fault lines. Terri's insistence that abuse was love isn't stupidity — it's the only frame that lets her keep her own past intact, and her repetition ("but he loved me… but he loved me") is the sound of someone defending a wound. Mel's flat certainty ("That's not love") reveals a man who needs definitions clean and can't tolerate the mess in his wife's history. Neither will move, because they're not arguing about Ed; they're arguing about whether the other one understands love at all. The dialogue dramatizes a relationship no narrator could summarize.

**In your own work:** Is there a word your characters would define differently, and does each definition protect something they can't afford to lose? Carver routes a whole marriage through an argument about what counts as "love," and lets neither side win — but the same pressure could play out over "home," "fair," "fine," or no single word at all. The thing to watch for is whether the disagreement is secretly about the people rather than the term; when it is, the argument can't resolve, and that irresolution is what carries the scene.

## The line that hides a confession: dialogue with a double bottom

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, subtext, tension, economy
**Source:** John le Carré, *The Looking Glass War* (1965) — Haldane, an intelligence officer, defining love during a conversation in the spy trade

> "Do you know what love is? I'll tell you: it is whatever you can still betray."

**Why it works:** On the surface it's an epigram about romance; underneath, it's an entire worldview and a confession of the speaker's profession and damage. A spy measures love not by devotion but by betrayability — the cynicism is total, and the chill comes from how reasonable he makes it sound. The line characterizes Haldane (a man who has made betrayal his medium) and indicts the institution he serves, all without naming either. It's dialogue that means more than it says and tells you what the speaker has become.

**In your own work:** When your character states a general truth about the world, what are they unknowingly confessing about themselves? Le Carré gives Haldane an epigram about love that turns out to be a spy's self-portrait, delivered calmly enough to chill — but a line need not be cynical or aphoristic to do this work, and yours might land softer or stranger. The principle is that a character's worldview is a confession in disguise; ask what your character has had to become in order to believe the thing they say so easily.

## Dialogue as moral confrontation: when conflict is the whole point

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, conflict, character, tension
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Emma* (1815) — Mr. Knightley rebukes Emma after she has publicly mocked the poor, garrulous Miss Bates at the Box Hill picnic

> "How could you be so unfeeling to Miss Bates? How could you be so insolent in your wit to a woman of her character, age, and situation?—Emma, I had not thought it possible. [...] She is poor; she has sunk from the comforts she was born to, and, if she live to old age, must probably sink more. Her situation should secure your compassion. It was badly done, indeed!"

**Why it works:** This is dialogue as a turning point, not decoration — it's the moment Emma's whole self-image shatters, and Austen delivers it as direct confrontation. Knightley's questions aren't rhetorical filler; the doubled "How could you" builds real pressure, and the plain verdict "It was badly done, indeed!" lands harder than any elaborate scolding because it's so short. The conflict reveals both: Knightley as the one person who will tell Emma the truth, and Emma (in her silence afterward, in the text) as someone capable of shame and growth. The cruelty was abstract until someone she respects names its human cost.

**In your own work:** When one character finally names another's harm, what makes the charge impossible to deflect? Austen builds pressure through Knightley's doubled questions and then drops it into one short, plain verdict — but the unanswerable note might come from quietness, or specificity, or who is speaking rather than from brevity. What's worth weighing is the rhythm: an accusation often lands hardest when its plainest line arrives after the longer ones, so the reader feels the words narrow to a point.

## Subtext inside a proposal: the line that insults while it adores

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, subtext, character, conflict
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — Mr. Darcy's first, disastrous proposal to Elizabeth Bennet

> "In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you."

**Why it works:** It sounds like passion, but listen to what's underneath: "In vain have I struggled… It will not do." Before he ever says he loves her, Darcy tells Elizabeth that loving her is a defeat — a thing he fought and lost. The proposal is simultaneously a declaration and an insult, because every word implies she is beneath him and his desire is a humiliation he's been forced to concede. The subtext is class contempt wearing the costume of ardor, which is exactly why she refuses him. The dialogue carries two opposite messages at once and trusts the reader (and Elizabeth) to hear the wrong one loud and clear.

**In your own work:** Could a single speech carry two opposite messages at once — the one the speaker intends and the one their word choice gives away? Darcy means to declare love, but "struggled," "in vain," "it will not do" leak the contempt underneath, and Austen trusts the reader to hear both; you might split the two messages along tone, timing, or what's left unsaid instead. The test is whether the betraying layer is visible to the reader while staying invisible to the speaker — the dramatic charge comes from that imbalance of awareness.

## Compression: a whole plot detonated in one reported sentence

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, economy, character, pacing
**Source:** George V. Higgins, *The Friends of Eddie Coyle* (1972) — the novel's first line, a near-all-dialogue Boston crime novel

> Jackie Brown, at twenty-six, with no expression on his face, said that he could get some guns.

**Why it works:** Higgins built a whole crime novel out of talk, and he opens by demonstrating how much a single reported line can carry. "At twenty-six" tells us he's young for this; "with no expression on his face" tells us he's already hardened or pretending to be; "said that he could get some guns" launches the entire plot's machinery — illegal arms, the men who want them, the betrayals to come — in nine words. By reporting the speech rather than quoting it, Higgins keeps the cool, transactional distance of the criminal world. The economy is the menace: no one in this book wastes a word, because words can get you killed.

**In your own work:** How much can a single reported line carry before it tips into overload — plot, character, and tone all at once? Higgins fires the whole novel's engine and fixes the speaker with two neutral details ("at twenty-six," "no expression on his face"), letting the coolness do the threatening — but reported speech is only one register, and a quoted line or a warmer voice might suit your world better. What's transferable is the economy: ask which few details would do double duty, advancing the story while revealing who's telling it, so nothing in the line is merely decorative.

## Dialogue as character on the page: the pompous letter that can't stop bowing

**Demonstrates:** dialogue, voice, character, subtext
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — Mr. Collins's letter introducing himself to the Bennets

> "...having received ordination at Easter, I have been so fortunate as to be distinguished by the patronage of the Right Honourable Lady Catherine de Bourgh [...] that the circumstance of my being next in the entail of Longbourn estate will be kindly overlooked on your side, and not lead you to reject the offered olive branch."

**Why it works:** Written speech is still dialogue, and Collins's sentences perform his entire character without a word of description. The clogged, self-congratulating syntax — every clause stuffed with deference to rank ("Right Honourable," "distinguished by the patronage") — reveals a man whose only values are status and self-importance. The "olive branch" flourish exposes the calculation underneath: he is angling to marry one of the daughters whose home he stands to inherit, and dressing greed as Christian peace-making. The voice is the man: servile upward, smug, and utterly unaware of how he sounds.

**In your own work:** What does your character's own sentence-making reveal that they would never admit? Austen lets Collins's clogged, deferential syntax and name-dropping expose the vanity and calculation he thinks he's hiding — but a voice can betray itself through curtness, jargon, evasion, or over-correction just as readily as through over-politeness. The principle is that style is character on the page; ask what your speaker's habitual way of forming sentences gives away about what they value and what they're managing to conceal.
