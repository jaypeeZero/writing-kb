# Character — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages: the actual text is embedded so you can study the move without owning the book. Each reveals character indirectly — through action, contradiction, desire, or voice — never through a label.

## Self-deception as character: the man who can't claim his own mistakes

**Demonstrates:** character, subtext, voice, show-dont-tell
**Source:** Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day* (1989) — Stevens, an aging butler, near the end of his life's reckoning

> You see, I trusted. I trusted his lordship's wisdom. All those years I served him, I trusted I was doing something worthwhile. I can't even say I made my own mistakes. Really—one has to ask oneself—what dignity is there in that?

**Why it works:** Stevens has spent the whole novel insisting on "dignity" as the highest professional virtue — and here, for one cracked moment, the construction fails and the cost surfaces. The character is revealed in the gap between his lifelong self-image (the dignified servant) and the buried truth (a man who surrendered his moral agency). Ishiguro doesn't tell us Stevens is tragic; he lets Stevens half-articulate it and then, characteristically, retreat. The hesitation — "Really—one has to ask oneself" — is the man trying to make even his grief impersonal.

**In your own work:** What is the self-justification your character has organized their life around, and what would it cost them to see past it? Ishiguro lets the construction crack for one moment and then has Stevens retreat into impersonal phrasing — but you might let yours look, or refuse to, or never get the chance. The principle to hold onto is that a defining belief reveals most at the seam where it almost fails; the question is whether your reader can feel the seam without you labeling it.

## Contradiction as character: the witty husband, the foolish wife

**Demonstrates:** character, voice, dialogue, subtext
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — Mr. Bennet answering his wife's complaint about her "poor nerves"

> "You mistake me, my dear. I have a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you mention them with consideration these last twenty years at least."

**Why it works:** Two characters land in two sentences. Mrs. Bennet is defined by what provokes the line — a lifetime of theatrical, self-pitying "nerves." Mr. Bennet is defined by the line itself: dry, detached, amused, and — crucially — *unkind underneath the wit*. "These last twenty years at least" reveals a marriage in which he copes with a wife he doesn't respect by making her the target of jokes she'll never get. The humor characterizes the speaker; the fact that he'd rather be clever than kind characterizes the marriage.

**In your own work:** What is your character's habitual verbal move, and what does it cost the people around them? Austen lets Mr. Bennet's wit charm the reader and indict the marriage in the same breath; that is one way to make a trait double back on its owner, not the only one. Worth testing: does the thing that makes your character likeable also quietly reveal their flaw, or are charm and fault sitting in separate compartments where the reader can keep them apart?

## Action as character: a man's whole life inside a suicide attempt

**Demonstrates:** character, detail-selection, show-dont-tell, economy
**Source:** Zadie Smith, *White Teeth* (2000) — the opening, introducing Archie Jones

> At 06.27 hours on 1 January 1975, Alfred Archibald Jones was dressed in corduroy and sat in a fume-filled Cavalier Musketeer Estate face down on the steering wheel, hoping the judgement would not be too heavy upon him. He lay in a prostrate cross, jaw slack, arms splayed either side like some fallen angel; scrunched up in each fist he held his army service medals (left) and his marriage licence (right), for he had decided to take his mistakes with him.

**Why it works:** We meet Archie at the lowest moment of his life and learn him entirely through objects and posture. The corduroy and the down-market car ("Cavalier Musketeer Estate") fix his class and unglamorousness; the medals and marriage licence clutched in each fist tell us what defines him (war, a failed marriage) and that he's a sentimentalist even in despair — "decided to take his mistakes with him." The comedy ("face down on the steering wheel") undercuts the tragedy and tells us the book's whole tone toward him: affectionate, ironic, forgiving. No adjectives of character; only evidence.

**In your own work:** If you stripped away every adjective of character, what would a reader still infer about your person from their posture, their surroundings, and the objects within reach? Smith hands Archie a down-market car and a fistful of medals and a marriage licence and lets us assemble the man — but the same principle works through a room, a gesture, a habit, and you might choose any of those. The test is whether the physical evidence is specific enough to carry class, history, and temperament on its own, without a narrator stepping in to vouch for them.

## Desire and cost as character: the consequence stated before the cause

**Demonstrates:** character, economy, show-dont-tell, tension
**Source:** Octavia E. Butler, *Kindred* (1979) — the opening lines; Dana, a Black woman in 1976, is pulled repeatedly back to an antebellum plantation

> I lost an arm on my last trip home. My left arm. And I lost about a year of my life and much of the comfort and security I had not valued until it was gone.

**Why it works:** The character is defined by what she's willing to lose. Butler opens with the bill, not the purchase — an amputation reported in a flat, almost administrative voice ("My left arm.") that tells us this is a woman who has been through too much to dramatize her own maiming. The understatement is the character: composed, durable, clear-eyed about cost. And the final clause — valuing comfort only "until it was gone" — quietly establishes the moral education the whole book delivers. We trust her instantly because she refuses to perform her own suffering.

**In your own work:** What has your character already lost, and how do they carry the telling of it? Butler reports an amputation in a flat administrative voice and lets the calm reveal the survivor — but the same gap could open the other way, with a small loss inflated or a large one stammered through, and either might fit your character better. What carries the weight is the distance between the size of the cost and the register in which it's reported; ask what that distance reveals about who is doing the reporting.

## The second-person "you" as a character portrait

**Demonstrates:** character, voice, subtext, economy
**Source:** Lorrie Moore, "How to Become a Writer," in *Self-Help* (1985) — a story told as mock-instructions to "you," an aspiring writer

> First, try to be something, anything, else.

**Why it works:** The whole character of the would-be writer is folded into one rueful imperative. The mock-advice form ("First, try...") promises a confident how-to, then immediately recommends giving up — and that gap between the brisk self-help voice and the defeated content *is* the character: someone who wants to write so badly they're already pre-grieving their failure, and who can only approach their own ambition through irony. The second person turns a private wound into a deadpan instruction, which is exactly how this character protects herself from feeling it.

**In your own work:** Is there a borrowed form — instructions, a recipe, an FAQ, a complaint letter — whose confident tone runs against the grain of what your character actually feels? Moore routes a writer's pre-grieved ambition through mock self-help, and the mismatch becomes the portrait; that is one container for the trick, and a dozen others would serve. The principle is that the gap between a form's promised attitude and its smuggled truth can stand in for a character's self-protection — so ask what your character would rather sound like than be.
