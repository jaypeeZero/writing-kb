# Story Frameworks — Worked Examples

Recognizable structural patterns — in medias res, tragedy, the hero's journey, frame stories, nested and parallel designs — shown in the works that execute them most cleanly.

## In medias res — the Odyssey starts in the middle of the wandering

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, pacing, premise, tension
**Source:** Homer, *The Odyssey* (c. 8th c. BCE; trans. Robert Fitzgerald, 1961) — the proem and the poem's late entry point into Odysseus's ten-year return

> Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story / of that man skilled in all ways of contending, / the wanderer, harried for years on end…

**Why it works / How it's built:** The poem does not begin with the fall of Troy or the start of the voyage; it opens with Odysseus already years into his ordeal, stranded on Calypso's island, while Telemachus searches for news of him at home. The famous early adventures — the Cyclops, the Lotus-Eaters, Circe — arrive later as Odysseus's own flashback narration at the court of the Phaeacians, so the chronology is deliberately folded. By entering "in the middle of things," Homer can open on stakes and longing rather than backstory, and the invocation tells us the whole scope in a few lines so the suspense is never "what happens" but "how he gets home." This is the template every later in-medias-res opening imitates.

**In your own work:** Where in your story's timeline does longing or danger run highest — and would starting there serve you better than starting at the chronological beginning? Homer opens years into the ordeal and folds the famous adventures back as Odysseus's own retelling; you might enter in the middle yet deliver the past some other way, or find that a linear opening suits your material. The question is what you want the reader's suspense to be about, since entering late shifts it from "what happens" toward "how it comes about."

## Tragedy: reversal and recognition fused — Oedipus the King (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, turning-point, arc, tension
**Source:** Sophocles, *Oedipus Rex* (c. 429 BCE) — Oedipus's investigation into the plague's cause becomes the investigation of himself

**Why it works / How it's built:** Aristotle held this up as the model tragedy because its turning point fuses *peripeteia* (reversal) and *anagnorisis* (recognition) into a single stroke. Oedipus's goal — find Laius's killer to lift the plague — is a detective's goal, and the structure is a tightening noose of testimony. The hinge is the Corinthian messenger who arrives meaning to *comfort* Oedipus (you are not Polybus's son, so no prophecy binds you) and instead delivers the fact that destroys him; the shepherd then confirms it. In one beat Oedipus's fortune reverses (king to outcast) and he recognizes the truth (he killed his father and married his mother) — the reversal *is* the recognition. The self-blinding is the falling action, an outward enactment of "painful ignorance to painful knowledge." Every piece of dramatic irony planted earlier pays off here.

**In your own work:** Could the thing your protagonist is searching for turn out to be themselves — and what happens if their reversal and their realization arrive in the same stroke? Sophocles fuses them so that the news meant to comfort Oedipus is the news that destroys him; you might keep recognition and reversal apart, letting one slowly produce the other. The consideration is whether your plot's central question implicates the person asking it, and whether collapsing the two beats intensifies the moment or robs it of aftermath you'd rather dramatize.

## The hero's journey — Luke Skywalker walks Campbell's monomyth (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, arc, turning-point, premise
**Source:** *Star Wars: A New Hope* (1977, dir. Lucas) — consciously built on Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*

**Why it works / How it's built:** Lucas mapped Campbell's stages onto the film almost beat for beat, which is why it reads as the textbook monomyth. Ordinary world: Luke as a restless farm boy on Tatooine. Call to adventure: R2-D2's message and Obi-Wan's invitation. Refusal of the call: Luke says he can't leave his aunt and uncle — until their murder removes the refusal. Mentor: Obi-Wan, the wise elder who hands over the lightsaber and the Force, then sacrifices himself (mentors must die so the hero can complete the journey alone). Crossing the threshold: the Mos Eisley cantina and leaving the planet. Tests/allies/enemies, the belly-of-the-whale Death Star rescue, the supreme ordeal of the trench run, and the return with the "elixir" (the Rebellion's victory). The framework gives mythic weight to a space adventure precisely because the audience recognizes the shape without naming it.

**In your own work:** Does the monomyth's spine — call, refusal, mentor, threshold — actually fit the story you're telling, or are you reaching for it because it's familiar? Star Wars maps it almost beat for beat and removes the mentor before the climax so the hero acts alone; that is one well-worn route, and stories that bend or abandon the shape can feel fresher for it. The test is whether each borrowed stage earns its place in your plot, and whether your protagonist is forced to stand unaided by the end regardless of which structure delivers them there.

## Frame story — a tenant hears the whole haunting secondhand — Wuthering Heights (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, premise, subtext, character
**Source:** Emily Brontë, *Wuthering Heights* (1847) — Lockwood records the housekeeper Nelly Dean's account of Heathcliff and Catherine

**Why it works / How it's built:** The novel is a story within a story. Lockwood, a baffled outsider renting Thrushcross Grange, sets the outer frame; almost the entire saga of Heathcliff and Catherine reaches us as Nelly Dean *tells* it to him, sometimes nested a further layer deep when Nelly quotes other characters' accounts. The frame does real structural work: it filters a wild, passionate story through two limited, biased narrators — Lockwood the obtuse newcomer and Nelly the partial servant — so the reader must read past both to reach the truth. The distance also lets Brontë open in the present (Lockwood's eerie first visit, the ghost at the window) and then reach backward, creating dread before the history that explains it. The framework converts melodrama into something ambiguous and unreliable.

**In your own work:** Who is telling your story, and what does their bias or blindness add or subtract? Brontë filters a wild passion through two limited narrators so the reader must read past both to reach the truth; you might use a single unreliable teller, or none at all if the material wants directness. The question is whether a frame buys you something real here — distance, dread, ambiguity, the reader's active interpretation — or whether it would only insulate the reader from a story that hits harder told straight.

## Nested Russian dolls — Cloud Atlas folds six stories inside each other (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, arc, premise, pacing
**Source:** David Mitchell, *Cloud Atlas* (2004) — six nested narratives across centuries, each interrupted and later resumed in reverse

**Why it works / How it's built:** Six stories spanning the 1800s to a post-apocalyptic future are each cut off mid-way and embedded inside the next, like matryoshka dolls, running 1-2-3-4-5-6 outward and then 6-5-4-3-2-1 back. The central sixth story is the only one told whole, and after it the earlier narratives close in reverse order. The nesting is literal and diegetic: each protagonist encounters the previous story as an artifact — a found journal, a packet of letters, a manuscript, a film, a recorded testimony — so the structure is also the plot's connective tissue. Mitchell pairs the design with a recurring comet-shaped birthmark and the epigraph idea that "a half-read book is a half-finished love affair," daring the reader to tolerate six cliffhangers before any resolution. The mirrored shape turns the book itself into an argument about recurrence and how stories outlive their tellers.

**In your own work:** If you nest or interrupt your stories, what binds the layers together beyond the bare fact of nesting? Mitchell breaks each strand at its peak and has each protagonist physically encounter the prior story as an artifact, so structure and plot become the same thing; you might connect your layers thematically rather than diegetically, or resist nesting where the cliffhangers would only frustrate. The thing to test is whether the design earns the reader's patience — whether the shape itself means something, rather than being cleverness the story has to survive.

## Parallel mosaic — A Visit from the Goon Squad tells one story through many (ANATOMY)

**Demonstrates:** story-frameworks, arc, pacing, character
**Source:** Jennifer Egan, *A Visit from the Goon Squad* (2010) — thirteen self-contained chapters, each a different protagonist, point of view, and moment in time

**Why it works / How it's built:** Modeled on a concept album, the book reads as thirteen near-standalone stories — different leads, voices, even formats (one chapter is rendered entirely as a teenage girl's PowerPoint slides) — that leap nonlinearly across the late 1970s to the near future. The unifying framework is a web of shared characters: a figure glimpsed in passing in one chapter becomes the center of another, decades earlier or later, so the reader assembles a single mosaic about time, music, and decay from scattered tiles. Egan refuses chronological order on purpose; the pleasure is recognition — realizing the minor assistant here is the broken mogul there. The parallel-lives framework lets her show the same "goon" (time) acting on a whole social network rather than one arc.

**In your own work:** Is your story really one arc, or a larger subject that no single protagonist can hold? Egan tells one mosaic about time through thirteen near-standalone pieces, binding them with figures who recur as minor here and central there; you might want fewer panels, a steadier point of view, or a chronological spine instead of scattered tiles. The consideration is whether the pleasure of recognition across pieces is strong enough to replace the momentum a single continuous arc would give you.
