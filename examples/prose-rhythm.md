# Prose Rhythm — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages with the real text embedded: each shows cadence at work — sentence-length variation, sound, repetition, and the way syntax can mimic what it describes.

## The plunge: a long sentence that swings open like the windows it describes

**Demonstrates:** prose-rhythm, show-dont-tell, voice
**Source:** Virginia Woolf, *Mrs. Dalloway* (1925) — Clarissa setting out into a June morning

> And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning — fresh as if issued to children on a beach. What a lark! What a plunge! For so it had always seemed to her when, with a little squeak of the hinges, which she could hear now, she had burst open the French windows and plunged at Bourton into the open air.

**Why it works:** Woolf sets two tiny exclamations — "What a lark! What a plunge!" — against the long, clause-stacked sentence that follows, and the contrast is the rhythm: the short cries are the impulse, the long unwinding sentence is the memory it triggers, syntactically diving back through time the way Clarissa "burst open" the windows. The sound enacts the sense; the sentence opens as the windows open. Cadence carries us inside her consciousness before any event occurs.

**In your own work:** Where might the shape of your sentences enact the motion they describe rather than just report it? Woolf sets two abrupt exclamations against a long, clause-stacked sentence so the short cries fire the impulse and the long one performs the dive into memory; you might find an altogether different pairing of lengths, or none at all. The question is whether the cadence carries us inside the character's consciousness before any event does.

## Anaphora as fog machine: rhythm built by relentless repetition

**Demonstrates:** prose-rhythm, economy, detail-selection
**Source:** Charles Dickens, *Bleak House* (1853) — the opening fog passage

> Fog on the Essex marshes, fog on the Kentish heights. Fog creeping into the cabooses of collier-brigs; fog lying out on the yards and hovering in the rigging of great ships; fog drooping on the gunwales of barges and small boats.

**Why it works:** Dickens drives the passage on anaphora — every clause launches from "fog" — and varies the verbs (creeping, lying, hovering, drooping) so the repetition never goes flat. The rhythm thickens as the participles pile up, and the prose physically performs the fog's spread: insistent, everywhere, slowing the eye the way fog slows the foot. The cadence, not the content, is what makes the weather oppressive.

**In your own work:** If repetition is building pressure in a passage, what keeps it from going flat? Dickens anchors every clause on "fog" but rotates the verbs, so the cadence thickens without deadening; that is one way to manage anaphora, and your scene might want the repetition broken, varied at the head instead of the verb, or abandoned. The thing to watch is whether the rhythm, not the content, is what produces the effect.

## In medias res in the present tense: terse beats that drop you onto the cobbles

**Demonstrates:** prose-rhythm, tension, show-dont-tell
**Source:** Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall* (2009) — the first lines, Thomas Cromwell beaten down by his father

> "So now get up."
> Felled, dazed, silent, he has fallen; knocked full length on the cobbles of the yard.

**Why it works:** Mantel opens on a four-word command, then a sentence front-loaded with three pounding past participles — "Felled, dazed, silent" — each landing like another kick before the verb arrives. The clipped, present-tense rhythm pins us in the now, and the asyndetic stack of adjectives mimics the rain of blows. The cadence is doing the violence; the meter is concussive.

**In your own work:** Can the meter of a sentence land like the blows it describes? Mantel stacks three stressed participles before the verb arrives, each landing like another kick, and keeps the rhythm clipped and present-tense; that is one concussive solution, and a different scene might want the violence stretched, slowed, or held at a distance. The consideration is whether the syntax is doing the violence, so the cadence carries what description alone would not.

## Two voices in one paragraph: the comma-loose climb up the hill

**Demonstrates:** prose-rhythm, detail-selection, voice
**Source:** Michael Ondaatje, *The English Patient* (1992) — the novel's opening

> She stands up in the garden where she has been working and looks into the distance. She has sensed a shift in the weather. There is another gust of wind, a buckle of noise in the air, and the tall cypresses sway. She turns and moves uphill toward the house, climbing over a low wall, feeling the first drops of rain on her bare arms.

**Why it works:** Ondaatje alternates a short declarative ("She has sensed a shift in the weather.") with a longer sentence that accrues by comma — "climbing over a low wall, feeling the first drops of rain" — so the rhythm itself rises and gusts like the weather it tracks. The participial phrases keep the final sentence in continuous motion, matching her walk uphill. Sentence length is choreographed to the body's movement and the air's change.

**In your own work:** Could the length and motion of your sentences be choreographed to a body's movement or a change in the air? Ondaatje alternates a short declarative with a sentence that accrues through trailing participles, so the rhythm rises and gusts like the weather it tracks; you might choreograph the prose to something else entirely, or keep the lengths even. The question is whether the variation in cadence is matched to what is actually moving in the scene.

## Monosyllables and a hard period: disbelief enacted by stopping and starting

**Demonstrates:** prose-rhythm, economy, voice
**Source:** James Baldwin, "Sonny's Blues" (1957) — the narrator reading of his brother's arrest

> I read about it in the paper, in the subway, on my way to work. I read it, and I couldn't believe it, and I read it again.

**Why it works:** Baldwin works almost entirely in monosyllables, and the rhythm comes from a triad of clipped prepositional phrases — "in the paper, in the subway, on my way to work" — that tick like a metronome before the hard period. The second sentence then loops with "and... and...," the cadence circling the way the narrator's eye circles back to the news. The beat of re-reading is built into the sentence's own returns.

**In your own work:** How would numb shock actually move through a sentence — and could the rhythm carry it without an adjective naming the feeling? Baldwin works in plain monosyllables and a looping "and... and" cadence that re-treads the same act, so the disbelief lives in the sentence's own returns; that is one way to render it, and another scene might fracture the rhythm rather than loop it. The consideration is whether the cadence itself conveys the state, so you need not announce it.
