# Economy — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages with the real text embedded: each wrings maximum meaning from minimum words, whether by compression, omission, or cold understatement.

## The bureaucratic euphemism that swallows a death

**Demonstrates:** economy, subtext, show-dont-tell, tension
**Source:** Raymond Carver, "Popular Mechanics," *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love* (1981) — the last lines of a story about two parents physically fighting over their baby

> She felt her fingers being forced open. She felt the baby going from her. No! she screamed just as her hands came loose. She would have it, this baby. She grabbed for the baby's other arm. She caught the baby around the wrist and leaned back. But he would not let go. He felt the baby slipping out of his hands and he pulled back very hard. In this manner, the issue was decided.

**Why it works:** The whole horror lands in the final cold sentence — "In this manner, the issue was decided" — which uses legal, passive, deliberately bloodless language to refuse the reader any image of what just happened to the child. Carver compresses a catastrophe into a euphemism, and the gap between the violence of the verbs ("forced," "pulled back very hard") and the dead calm of the closing clause is where the meaning detonates. Saying less makes it worse.

**In your own work:** At the peak of a brutal moment, how much do you actually need to show? Carver pulls back into flat, legal language — "the issue was decided" — and lets the gap between the violent verbs and the dead-calm close do the damage; you might withhold differently, or decide your scene needs the result on the page. The thing to weigh is whether saying less makes it worse here, and whether the reader has enough to supply what you have refused.

## A universal truth in one ironic sentence

**Demonstrates:** economy, voice, subtext
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — the novel's first sentence

> It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

**Why it works:** Austen establishes her entire social satire in twenty-three words by mounting a grand, mock-philosophical frame ("a truth universally acknowledged") over a petty marriage-market calculation, so the sentence states the opposite of what it pretends. The economy is in the irony: a single deadpan generalization characterizes a whole society's obsessions, signals the narrator's wit, and previews the plot. Nothing needs unpacking because the tone has already told you not to trust the surface.

**In your own work:** Can a single sentence carry both your narrator's attitude and your story's subject? Austen mounts a grand, mock-philosophical frame over a petty marriage-market calculation, and the mismatch in pitch does the ironic work; that is one route, and irony pitched "too grand" is only one of many tonal mismatches available. The question is whether the tone alone tells the reader not to trust the surface, so nothing has to be unpacked.

## Fog without a verb: weather rendered by accumulation, not explanation

**Demonstrates:** economy, prose-rhythm, detail-selection
**Source:** Charles Dickens, *Bleak House* (1853) — the opening fog passage

> Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city.

**Why it works:** Dickens drops the main verb entirely — "Fog everywhere" is not a sentence, it's a condition — and then builds by bare repetition of "fog" rather than describing it. The economy is structural: by refusing finite verbs and simply naming the fog in place after place, he makes the prose itself feel choked and pervasive. The word does the work that a paragraph of explanation would only dilute.

**In your own work:** When one element needs to flood a scene, will describing it dilute the very saturation you want? Dickens drops the finite verbs and simply names the fog place after place, so the grammar starves and the atmosphere thickens; you might instead lean on description, or pick a different element to repeat. The consideration is whether the structure of the prose can enact the condition rather than explain it.

## Reading reduced to instruction: prose that does its job in imperatives

**Demonstrates:** economy, voice, opening-lines
**Source:** Italo Calvino, *If on a winter's night a traveler* (1979, trans. William Weaver) — the novel's opening

> You are about to begin reading Italo Calvino's new novel, *If on a winter's night a traveler*. Relax. Concentrate. Dispel every other thought. Let the world around you fade.

**Why it works:** Calvino strips the opening to bare imperatives — "Relax. Concentrate." — single words functioning as whole sentences, so the prose enacts the act of settling into a book by issuing it as commands. The economy is the joke and the method: a novel about reading begins by spending almost no words, just clipped instructions, drawing the reader into the second person before any "story" exists. Maximum address, minimum apparatus.

**In your own work:** Could your prose perform an action instead of describing it, and how close to the reader does that bring you? Calvino collapses the distance with bare one-word imperatives that enact the act of settling into a book; that is one way to use economy as method, and your scene may want the distance kept, not collapsed. The thing to test is whether the clipped form is doing the work — maximum address, minimum apparatus — or whether you are spending words to say what the form could simply do.
