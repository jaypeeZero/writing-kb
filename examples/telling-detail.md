# The Telling Detail — Worked Examples

Self-contained passages: the actual text is embedded so you can study the move without owning the book. Each shows a single concrete detail (or a catalogue of them) carrying weight that summary never could.

## Cataloguing as characterization: the inventory that hides a death

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, show-dont-tell, character, economy
**Source:** Tim O'Brien, *The Things They Carried* (1990) — the title story, which defines each soldier by the objects he hauls

> Ted Lavender, who was scared, carried tranquilizers until he was shot in the head outside the village of Than Khe in mid-April.

> Henry Dobbins, who was a big man, carried extra rations; he was especially fond of canned peaches in heavy syrup over pound cake.

**Why it works:** The whole story is built as an inventory — each man named, then defined by what he carries. The objects do the characterization: fear becomes tranquilizers, size becomes extra rations and a sweet tooth. The Lavender sentence is the masterstroke — it buries a man's death mid-clause, in the same flat logistical register as the canned peaches, as if his being "shot in the head" were one more item on the packing list. The deliberate flatness *is* the trauma: this is how soldiers metabolize death, by filing it. Synecdoche throughout — the load stands in for the man.

**In your own work:** Could the objects your characters carry, keep, or refuse to discard stand in for the people themselves? O'Brien defines each soldier by his load and then buries a death mid-clause, in the same flat logistical register as canned peaches, so the blow lands before the reader can brace — but the catalogue is one container for this, and a single character's possessions or a single charged object could do similar work. What's worth weighing is the register: when devastating information arrives in the same calm syntax as the trivia, the flatness itself becomes the meaning, telling you how these people metabolize what they can't face.

## The object that won't be given away

**Demonstrates:** detail-selection, show-dont-tell, subtext
**Source:** Joan Didion, *The Year of Magical Thinking* (2005) — on the death of her husband

> I could not give away the rest of his shoes. I stood there for a moment, then realized why: he would need shoes if he was to return.

**Why it works:** The word "grief" never appears. The grief *is* the irrational thought — keeping a dead man's shoes because the alternative is admitting he won't come back. Didion reports the reasoning deadpan, in the same plain sentences she'd use for anything else, and the gap between the calm telling and the impossible belief underneath is where the feeling lives. One concrete object, the shoes, holds the entire unspeakable thing.

**In your own work:** Is there one physical object and one irrational decision about it that could hold an emotion you'd otherwise have to name? Didion locates grief in the shoes she can't give away and the impossible reasoning behind keeping them, reported deadpan so the gap between calm telling and impossible belief carries the feeling; the emotion might be love or fear or shame instead, and the decision might be to keep, discard, or guard. The principle is that a reader infers feeling more powerfully from an act than from a label, so it's worth asking which single object and single choice would let you leave the emotion unnamed.
