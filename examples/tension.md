# Tension — Worked Examples

Suspense, dread, dramatic irony, and the unanswered question: passages where the actual text generates pressure, plus anatomies of how a work sustains it.

## Dramatic irony as a self-laid trap: *Oedipus Rex*

**Demonstrates:** tension, dramatic-irony, stakes, turning-point
**Source:** Sophocles, *Oedipus Rex* (c. 429 BCE) — Oedipus vows to hunt down the murderer of King Laius, whose death has cursed Thebes with plague

**How it's built:** The audience knows from the start what Oedipus does not: that he himself killed Laius, his father, and married his mother. Sophocles weaponizes that gap. Oedipus publicly curses the unknown murderer — "may he wear out his life in misery" — and pledges to fight for the dead king "as if for my own father," lines that detonate because the crowd hears him condemning himself. Every confident vow tightens the noose; the tension is not "what happened?" but "when will he see what we already see, and how much will it cost?" Jocasta's attempts to comfort him ("a man should live at random") supply more evidence to the audience while soothing the character.

**In your own work:** What does your reader know that your protagonist does not? Sophocles hands the audience the whole secret up front, then lets Oedipus condemn himself with every confident vow — but that gap can be wide or narrow, opened early or late, and ignorance need not be total. The question is whether the character's boldness is something the reader can read as self-entrapment.

## The threat that never names itself: *We Have Always Lived in the Castle*

**Demonstrates:** tension, dread, subtext, the-unanswered-question
**Source:** Shirley Jackson, *We Have Always Lived in the Castle* (1962) — the novel's opening paragraph, narrated by eighteen-year-old Merricat Blackwood

> My name is Mary Katherine Blackwood. I am eighteen years old, and I live with my sister Constance. I have often thought that with any luck at all I could have been born a werewolf, because the two middle fingers on both my hands are the same length, but I have had to be content with what I had. I dislike washing myself, and dogs, and noise. I like my sister Constance, and Richard Plantagenet, and Amanita phalloides, the death-cup mushroom. Everyone else in my family is dead.

**How it's built:** Jackson buries a lethal threat inside a child's list of likes and dislikes. "Amanita phalloides, the death-cup mushroom" sits between two cozy items, and the flat final sentence — "Everyone else in my family is dead" — is dropped without affect, raising the unanswered question that powers the whole book: *how did they die, and what does Merricat have to do with the poison she just mentioned?* The dread comes from tone, not event; the narrator's serene fondness for a deadly mushroom tells us more than any description of murder could.

**In your own work:** Where could menace hide in plain, untroubled language? Jackson sets a death-cup mushroom between two cozy likes and reports a family's death without affect, leaving the reader to draw the line she withholds — but tonal flatness is one route to dread, and the unanswered connection is one kind of hook. Consider whether the gap between your narrator's calm and the reader's alarm is doing the work, or whether you're merely concealing information.

## Swapped murders, asymmetric knowledge: *Strangers on a Train*

**Demonstrates:** tension, dramatic-irony, stakes, dread
**Source:** Patricia Highsmith, *Strangers on a Train* (1950) — Bruno proposes to Guy that they "exchange" murders, each killing the other's burden so neither has a motive

**How it's built:** Highsmith builds tension on a single sick premise: Bruno takes a hypothetical chat as a binding contract, murders Guy's estranged wife, then waits — and begins pressuring Guy to fulfill "his half." The reader is trapped in Guy's escalating dread because we know Bruno is serious long before Guy can admit it, and we know that the more Guy resists, the more Bruno will not let go. The suspense is moral, not procedural: not "will Guy get caught?" but "will Guy do the unthinkable, and what is the slow erosion that might make him?" Bruno's casual intimacy — phone calls, gifts, showing up — turns dread domestic.

**In your own work:** Can your pressure come from a bind rather than a chase? Highsmith has one man treat a hypothetical as a contract and act alone, trapping the other in a crime he never agreed to — but the asymmetry could be any obligation one character feels and another denies. Ask whether the dread is moral (what might this person become?) rather than procedural (will they get caught?), since the former rarely needs a chase scene at all.

## The unanswered question as engine: *Rebecca*

**Demonstrates:** tension, the-unanswered-question, dread, subtext
**Source:** Daphne du Maurier, *Rebecca* (1938) — the unnamed second Mrs. de Winter arrives at Manderley, haunted by her predecessor

> Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again.

**How it's built:** Du Maurier withholds answers as a structural method. The narrator has no name; the dead first wife, Rebecca, is everywhere — her handwriting, her bedroom kept intact, her devoted housekeeper Mrs. Danvers — yet the central question ("what was Rebecca really, and what happened to her?") is starved chapter by chapter. The dreamy opening line, with its haunted "again," promises that the past is inescapable before we know what the past contains. Tension comes from a vacuum: the narrator (and reader) measure themselves against a woman they cannot see and cannot stop hearing about, and every kindness at Manderley might be a threat.

**In your own work:** Is there an absence your protagonist measures themselves against? Du Maurier saturates Manderley with the dead Rebecca's traces while starving the central question of an answer, so dread grows from comparison and ignorance — but the absent figure could be a person, an event, a former self, and the question can be rationed at any rate. The test is whether the vacuum keeps generating pressure, or whether the reader stops caring what fills it.

## Suspense from a ticking clock and a withheld fact: "The Lottery" pressure, done elsewhere — *The Silence of the Lambs*

**Demonstrates:** tension, stakes, dramatic-irony, pacing
**Source:** Thomas Harris, *The Silence of the Lambs* (1988) — trainee Clarice Starling races to identify serial killer "Buffalo Bill" before he kills his captive, Catherine Martin

**How it's built:** Harris runs two clocks at once. The reader is shown Buffalo Bill and his victim alive in the pit (knowledge Clarice lacks), so we feel the countdown she can't see — classic dramatic irony layered over a literal deadline. Meanwhile Hannibal Lecter doles out clues in cryptic riddles, each one delaying gratification while raising the stakes, so the path to the answer is itself a source of dread. The famous reveal that the rescue raid is at the wrong house — the agents breaking down an empty door while Clarice, alone, walks toward the real one — is a pure suspense engineering trick: split the reader's hope from the protagonist's position.

**In your own work:** What does the reader see that your protagonist cannot, and is there a clock on it? Harris runs a literal deadline under dramatic irony, then at the climax splits where the rescue believes it is from where the danger actually is — but you might run one clock or two, and the misdirection is one way to fire relief and terror together. Consider whether separating the reader's hope from the protagonist's position would sharpen the climax or merely complicate it.
