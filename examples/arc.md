# Arc — Worked Examples

Character and emotional change across a whole work: anatomies of the before/after and the want-vs-need split, naming the actual beats, with verified text at the hinge points.

## Want vs. need, and the cost of never closing the gap: *The Remains of the Day*

**Demonstrates:** arc, want, turning-point, subtext, stakes
**Source:** Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day* (1989) — butler Stevens drives across England to see Miss Kenton, twenty years after they worked together at Darlington Hall

**Anatomy:** Stevens's *want* is plausible and small — to persuade Miss Kenton to return to staff, framed entirely as a professional errand. His *need*, which he cannot name, is to admit he loved her and to face that his life of perfect "dignity" served a Nazi-sympathizing master and cost him every human thing. The arc is built from his evasions: the night his father dies, he keeps serving dinner; the night Miss Kenton waits for a word from him, he discusses the silver. The hinge comes at their reunion, when she says she has wondered about "a different life" — and Stevens finally lets one true sentence escape: "at that moment, my heart was breaking." It is the only direct admission in the book, and it comes too late. The arc is a tragedy of recognition without redemption: he changes only enough to know what he wasted.

**In your own work:** Where is the gap between what your character chases and what would actually heal them? Ishiguro closes it with recognition that comes too late; you might close it some other way. The test is whether the want is concrete enough to hide the need — from the reader and from the character both.

## The full redemptive reversal, beat by beat: *A Christmas Carol*

**Demonstrates:** arc, turning-point, before-after, stakes
**Source:** Charles Dickens, *A Christmas Carol* (1843) — the miser Ebenezer Scrooge across one supernatural night

**Anatomy:** Dickens engineers the cleanest before/after in English fiction with a tight machinery of beats. **Before:** Scrooge refuses Christmas, underpays Bob Cratchit, dismisses the poor ("Are there no prisons?"). **Catalyst:** Marley's ghost, chained by his own greed, warns him. **Escalation:** the three spirits stage a structured argument — Past (Belle, the fiancée he lost to money; his lonely boyhood) cracks his self-image; Present (the Cratchits' joy despite poverty, Tiny Tim's empty chair foreshadowed) cracks his heart; Future (his own unmourned death, his stolen bed-curtains) cracks his nerve. **Turn:** at the graveside he begs, "I am not the man I was." **After:** he wakes, buys the prize turkey, raises Cratchit's salary, becomes "a second father" to Tim. Each ghost targets a different faculty — memory, empathy, fear — so the conversion is argued, not asserted.

**In your own work:** What would it take for your reader to feel a change of heart was earned rather than announced? Dickens stages it as an argument aimed in turn at memory, empathy, and fear — each ghost a different faculty — but that is one architecture, not the only one. Consider which of your character's faculties actually resist the change, and whether your turn engages those or merely declares the outcome.

## The slow correction of a flaw named in the title: *Pride and Prejudice*

**Demonstrates:** arc, want, turning-point, subtext
**Source:** Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) — Elizabeth Bennet and Fitzwilliam Darcy

**Anatomy:** Elizabeth's arc is the dismantling of her own prejudice; Darcy's is the humbling of his pride, and Austen braids them so each one's change enables the other's. Elizabeth *wants* to be right — about Darcy's arrogance, about Wickham's charm — and her *need* is to learn that her quick judgment is itself a vanity. The hinge is Darcy's letter after his rejected first proposal: reading and re-reading it, she realizes she has misjudged everyone, and says aloud, "Till this moment, I never knew myself." That is the turning-point of self-recognition; everything after is her revising her evidence (Pemberley, the housekeeper's praise, the Lydia rescue) and Darcy revising his manner. The famous opening — "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife" — is ironic precisely because the book will show that pairing rightly requires both parties to *change*, not merely to match fortunes.

**In your own work:** What is your protagonist so sure of that it has become a blind spot? Austen makes Elizabeth's flaw a confidence in her own judgment, then turns it on a single piece of new evidence that forces a re-reading of everything — but a flaw need not be certainty, and the reckoning need not be one letter. The question is whether the thing that has to change is also the thing the character most trusts about themselves.

## A descent arc — sympathetic man to monster: Walter White, *Breaking Bad*

**Demonstrates:** arc, want, stakes, turning-point
**Source:** Vince Gilligan (creator), *Breaking Bad* (2008–2013) — chemistry teacher Walter White after a terminal cancer diagnosis

**Anatomy:** Gilligan pitched it as turning "Mr. Chips into Scarface," and the arc is a negative one executed in measured steps. **Want:** Walt's stated goal is to provide for his family before he dies — the lie that licenses everything. **Need (refused):** to accept help and die with humility; instead he chooses pride. The beats escalate the moral cost: letting Jane choke to death (Season 2) to protect his partnership; poisoning a child to manipulate Jesse (Season 4); ordering the prison murders (Season 5). The arc's hinge is the finale's confession — for four seasons he claims "I did it for my family," then finally admits, "I did it for me. I liked it. I was good at it. And I was really alive." The before/after is total: the man who couldn't bear charity in the pilot dies having become the danger he once feared.

**In your own work:** In a corruption arc, what lie does your character tell themselves to license the descent? Gilligan lets Walt keep "I did it for my family" until a final admission that the real motive was appetite — but you might let the mask slip earlier, or never fully, or have someone else strip it. Ask how long the justification can hold before it strains credibility, and what is gained by holding it that long.

## Earning the happy ending through hard-won equality: *Jane Eyre*

**Demonstrates:** arc, want, need, stakes
**Source:** Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre* (1847) — orphan, governess, and her employer Rochester

**Anatomy:** Jane's arc moves from powerless dependence to chosen, equal love, and Brontë refuses to let her shortcut it. Jane *wants* love and belonging — first from the Reeds who reject her, then from Rochester. Her *need* is self-respect that doesn't require self-erasure. The pivotal refusal is structural: on discovering Rochester's mad wife in the attic on her wedding day, Jane leaves the man she loves rather than become his mistress — "I care for myself" — choosing dignity over the very belonging she craved. Only after she gains independence (the inheritance, the refusal of St. John's loveless proposal) and Rochester is humbled — blinded, widowed, no longer her master — can she return as an equal. The closing "Reader, I married him" lands as a triumph because the marriage is now between equals, not a rescue. The arc is the journey from needing love at any cost to accepting it only on terms that keep the self intact.

**In your own work:** How do you keep a happy ending from reading as a rescue rather than a choice? Brontë has Jane refuse the thing she most wants on principle, then return only as an equal — but a refusal at the midpoint is one way to earn that equality, not the only one. Consider what would have to change in your character, or in their circumstances, for the eventual "yes" to read as self-possession.
